Capturing and dissecting wireless traffic with Wireshark

💭THINK FIRST · WLAN ANALYSIS▾

WLANs use CSMA/CA and radio frequency signals. Spectrum analyzers and Wireshark together give a full picture — spectrum shows raw RF energy, Wireshark shows decoded packets.

  • If users on a WLAN have trouble connecting to wired servers, where would you start your analysis — the WLAN or wired side? Why?
  • What is the difference between promiscuous mode and monitor mode on a WLAN adapter, and why does monitor mode matter for capture?
Q1: Start on the wired side first: if the STA SYN packet never arrives on the wired network, the problem is on the WLAN. This lets you quickly rule out wired issues before investing time on wireless capture.
Q2: Promiscuous mode captures all traffic on the joined SSID. Monitor mode (rfmon) does not associate with any SSID — it passively captures all frames on the selected channel from all SSIDs, including management and control frames.

Analyze WLAN Traffic

Wireless LANs (WLANs) are based on the IEEE 802.11 standard. One key trait of 802.11 WLANs is that they use Carrier Sense (every WLAN host is listening), Multiple Access (shared medium) and Collision Avoidance — or CSMA/CA protocol. As you analyze network traffic, it is important to know what normal looks like: normal signal strength values, normal RF signals in the area (without interference), normal association processes, normal data exchange over 802.11, and normal disassociation. Performing a site survey and including trace files in the process can provide a valuable baseline of WLAN traffic.

Rule Out the Wired Network to Point to the WLAN

You can identify some WLAN problems when you have only tapped into the wired network. If you do not see the WLAN station SYN packet (in an attempt to establish a connection to the server on the wired network), then something is happening on the WLAN side. Think of a Basic Service Set (BSS) as a subnet controlled by the access point (AP). Each BSS has an identification (ID) value based on the MAC address. When you communicate from the wireless network to an Ethernet wired network, the associated AP strips off the 802.11 header and encapsulates your packet into an Ethernet frame before forwarding onto the wired network.

Analyze Signal Strength and Interference

WLAN networks are dependent upon the strength of radio frequency (RF) signals. As a wireless signal travels, its signal strength diminishes. Other RF energy transmitting on the same frequency from other devices can interfere with the signals. One of the most valuable tools used to examine WLAN signals is a spectrum analyzer. Without a spectrum analyzer, you are blind to RF interference — it is impossible to see and diagnose these issues properly with any other tool. Wireshark cannot identify, capture or display RF energy unless it is modulated as 802.11 frames. Spectrum analyzers cannot capture and display packets — they only display raw RF energy. The two technologies working together offer the best option for troubleshooting wireless networks.

💭THINK FIRST · WLAN CAPTURE MODES▾

Capturing WLAN traffic requires choosing the right adapter mode. Monitor mode sees all SSIDs and management/control frames; promiscuous mode only sees the joined SSID.

  • Why would you want to capture WLAN traffic directly on the host you are investigating rather than using a shared medium capture?
  • What are the consequences of using WEP vs WPA decryption in Wireshark, particularly around the EAPOL handshake?
Q1: If you cannot capture in monitor mode, you must run Wireshark directly on the target host — you will not see traffic destined to other devices. This limits troubleshooting of multi-host issues.
Q2: WEP can be decrypted directly with just the key. WPA/WPA2 requires that you capture the four-packet EAPOL handshake during the association process — if you miss it, you cannot decrypt that session retroactively.

Capture WLAN Traffic

When you analyze WLAN traffic using Wireshark, get as close as you can to the complaining user. In promiscuous mode, an 802.11 adapter only captures packets of the SSID the adapter has joined. In monitor mode (rfmon), the driver does not make the adapter a member of any service set — all packets from all SSIDs on the currently selected channel are captured. Monitor mode is not supported by WinPcap, limiting WLAN analysis capabilities of Wireshark on Windows.

Set Up WLAN Decryption

You must have the decryption key in order to decrypt WLAN traffic. Decryption keys can be input using the Decryption Mode and Decryption Key Management on the Wireless Toolbar or in the IEEE 802.11 preferences setting. Wireshark can decrypt WEP, WPA and WPA2 traffic. When decrypting WPA traffic, you must capture the four packet EAPOL (Extensible Authorization Protocol) packets. If you specify Driver mode, you can only use WEP keys to decrypt the traffic.

Select Radiotap or PPI Headers

You have three choices in WLAN settings for headers: 802.11 header only, Prepend a Radiotap pseudoheader, or Prepend a PPI (Per-Packet Information) pseudoheader. Use Wireless Settings on the Wireless Toolbar to define the header. The Radiotap and PPI pseudoheaders provide additional information about frames received — specifically signal strength, channel/frequency, and data rate. You must apply the Radiotap or PPI header in order to filter on the 802.11 channel/frequency information.

Compare Signal Strength and Signal-to-Noise Ratios

The signal strength indicator value defines the power (in dBm). From 0 to -65 dBm is considered excellent to acceptable; problems will likely occur if the signal strength goes below -80 dBm. The signal-to-noise ratio defines the difference between the signal and noise values. Higher ratio numbers indicate less noise obstruction. If this value reaches less than 15 dB, performance is degraded.

💭THINK FIRST · 802.11 FRAME TYPES▾

802.11 has three frame types: data, management, and control. Management frames handle association/authentication; control frames handle delivery of other frames.

  • What happens if beacon frames stop appearing at their expected 100ms interval — and how would you diagnose whether the AP stopped beaconing or your capture point simply missed them?
  • Why can only data frames be forwarded from the wireless network to the wired Ethernet network?
Q1: Create an IO Graph filtered on wlan.fc.type_subtype==0x08 (beacons). If gaps appear, deploy a second analyzer at the same AP to confirm whether the AP truly stopped beaconing or if your capture point lost signal.
Q2: Management and control frames are MAC-layer constructs used to manage wireless connectivity. The wired Ethernet standard does not understand 802.11-specific fields, so the AP strips the 802.11 header and applies an Ethernet header before forwarding only data frames.

Understand 802.11 Traffic Basics

There are three types of 802.11 frames seen on WLANs: Data (contains data, the only type forwarded to the wired network), Management (used to establish MAC-layer connectivity — Association Request/Responses, Probe Requests/Responses and Beacons), and Control (used to enable delivery of data and management frames — RTS, CTS and ACKs).

Management Frames

  • Authentication: STA sends to AP with identity; AP responds indicating success or failure
  • Deauthentication: STA sends to terminate secure communications
  • Association: Used by AP to synchronize with STA radio and define capabilities
  • Reassociation: Sent by STA to new AP; triggers AP to get buffered data from previous AP
  • Disassociation: Sent by STA to terminate an association with the AP
  • Beacon: Sent every 100 ms (default) by AP to announce its presence and provide info
  • Probe: Request/Response — STA uses to obtain info from another STA; e.g., find APs in range

One of the most important management frames on the WLAN is the beacon frame. If users complain about intermittent loss of connectivity, consider creating an IO Graph using a filter for beacon frames. These frames should occur at an interval of approximately 100ms.

Control Frames

  • Request-to-Send (RTS): Optional — used as part of 2-way handshake to request transmission privileges
  • Clear-to-Send (CTS): Optional — second part of 2-way handshake
  • ACK: Sent by receiver to indicate data frame received OK. No ACK triggers an 802.11 retransmission by the sender

Analyze Normal 802.11 Communications

When a station wants to access a wireless network, it must connect with an AP that supports the desired SSID. The station can either wait (passive mode) for a beacon frame or send a probe request to find the AP (active mode). Connection to a WLAN requires that the STA (a) decide which AP to join, (b) successfully complete the authentication process, and (c) successfully complete the association process.

💭THINK FIRST · 802.11 FRAME STRUCTURE▾

The 802.11 Frame Control field is only 2 bytes but contains many single-bit flags including Retry, Power Management, Protected Frame, and the critical To DS / From DS addressing bits.

  • How do the To DS and From DS bits indicate whether traffic is within a BSS, going to the wired network, or between two APs?
  • When filtering on wlan.fc.type_subtype, why do you need to convert the type/subtype values to hex?
Q1: To DS=0, From DS=0 = both stations in the same BSS. To DS=0, From DS=1 = traffic from the DS (AP to STA). To DS=1, From DS=0 = STA to the wired network. To DS=1, From DS=1 = between distribution systems (AP to AP).
Q2: Wireshark wlan.fc.type_subtype field uses decimal values converted from a 6-bit binary field. Converting to hex (e.g. probe response = 000101 = 0x05) simplifies filter writing and avoids errors with complex bitfields.

Dissect the 802.11 Frame Structure

802.11 headers contain much more information than a simple Ethernet header. An 802.11 association frame contains 17 fields in the header. Many fields are only a single bit long. The frame body is variable length; the maximum size varies depending on encryption type: WEP adds 8 bytes (2,312 total), WPA TKIP adds 20 bytes (2,324 total), CCMP/WPA2 adds 16 bytes (2,320 total).

Case Studies

Case Study 1
Cruddy Barcode Communications
Submitted by: Vik Evans

Vik worked as a wireless engineer for a large shipping company with wireless deployments at hundreds of sites for scanning packages. After a wireless switch OS upgrade, sites began reporting that scanner screen refresh was taking 7-10 seconds instead of the normal 1-3 seconds.

Capturing on the switch ETH port, Vik found that the server was sending refresh data as single-character 64-byte packets. Because the first packet acknowledgment was missed, the server eventually retransmitted the entire data as a single 1500-byte packet — which the scanner received successfully. The 7-10 second delay was the server retransmission timeout.

The root cause: the scanner was going into sleep mode and not returning to a ready state in time to receive the first packet. The OS upgrade had improved switching speed enough to expose this long-standing bug. The sys-admin configured the server to buffer data into single 1500-byte packets, resolving the issue.

Case Study 2
Cooking the WLAN
Submitted by: Mark Jensen, Product Manager at MetaGeek

A large seafood distributor experienced WLAN connectivity drops every hour for about ten minutes, then perfect restoration. A spectrum analyzer (Wi-Spy / Chanalyzer) was used to investigate.

Walking the warehouse floor, the spectrum looked nearly ideal — until a massive RF energy spike appeared across the entire 2.4 GHz band. Following the noise with Chanalyzer Waterfall View led to the break room, where all five microwave ovens were running simultaneously during hourly breaks.

Ditching the microwaves was not viable. Since office staff had recently refreshed to 5 GHz laptops, the solution was to steer affected users to the 802.11a/n SSID, completely avoiding 2.4 GHz interference.

TL;DR — Chapter 26 Summary

Wireless networks (WLANs) are based on the IEEE 802.11 standard and rely on radio frequency (RF) signals. WLANs use CSMA/CA — Carrier Sense, Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance. The integrity and strength of RF signals should be evaluated with a spectrum analyzer when WLAN problems occur.

In a typical WLAN environment, traffic flows between stations (STAs) and access points (APs). Ideally, your Wireshark WLAN interface should support both promiscuous and monitor mode operations to capture traffic to and from other devices, regardless of the SSID they are associated with. WLAN traffic can be encrypted in WEP, WPA, or WPA2.

Wireshark can prepend two different types of pseudoheaders onto the 802.11 header — a Radiotap header or a PPI header — providing additional information about frames received including signal strength, channel/frequency, and data rate. There are three WLAN traffic types: control, management and data.

Review Questions

Q26.1. What does an Access Point do with the 802.11 header when it forwards a packet onto an Ethernet network?
A: The access point strips off the 802.11 header and applies an Ethernet header before forwarding the packet on.
Q26.2. What tool can be used to identify RF interference and RF energy?
A: A spectrum analyzer, such as Wi-Spy and Chanalyzer Pro, offers insight into RF interference and RF energy.
Q26.3. What is monitor mode?
A: In monitor mode, an adapter does not associate with any SSID — all packets from all SSIDs on the selected channel are captured.
Q26.4. What are your capture limitations if the WLAN adapter does not support promiscuous mode?
A: If an adapter does not support promiscuous mode, you cannot listen to traffic destined to other hardware addresses. You must capture traffic directly on the host you are interested in.
Q26.5. What graph can you create to verify access point availability?
A: Create an IO Graph and add a filter for beacon frames (wlan.fc.type_subtype==0x08) to verify access point availability.
Q26.6. What is the advantage of prepending a Radiotap header to your 802.11 traffic?
A: The Radiotap header contains the radiotap.channel.freq field. Prepending this header enables you to filter on the WLAN channel.
Q26.7. What are the three WLAN traffic types used to transport data, establish MAC-layer connectivity and enable delivery of frames?
A: The three WLAN traffic types are data, management and control.
Q26.8. What is the purpose of Association frames on a WLAN?
A: Association request and response frames are sent by stations to synchronize with the access point and exchange capability information.
Q26.9. What is the default interval of WLAN beacon frames?
A: By default, beacon frames are sent approximately every 100ms.
