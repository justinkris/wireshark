💭 THINK FIRST · 802.11 WLAN OVERVIEW

Wireless analysis requires a fundamental mindset shift from wired: you can't see all traffic without monitor mode, the frame format has additional fields for wireless medium access, and management frames control the connection lifecycle in ways Ethernet never needed.

  • You start a Wireshark capture on your Wi-Fi interface but only see your own traffic. What change do you need to make to capture all wireless traffic in the area?
  • Why does 802.11 need four address fields in its frame header when Ethernet only needs two?
Q1 — ENABLING MONITOR MODE
You need to enable Monitor Mode on the wireless interface. In managed (infrastructure) mode, the NIC only captures frames addressed to your own MAC or broadcast/multicast. Monitor mode puts the NIC in a promiscuous wireless mode where it captures all frames on the channel regardless of destination MAC. On macOS use airport utility or Wireshark's native monitor mode; on Linux use airmon-ng; on Windows monitor mode support varies by NIC and driver. Note: in monitor mode you lose your own network connection (the NIC is no longer associated to an AP).
Q2 — FOUR ADDRESS FIELDS IN 802.11
802.11 infrastructure networks use up to four addresses because wireless frames may need to traverse both wireless and wired segments. The four are: (1) Receiver Address (RA) — the next-hop 802.11 receiver, (2) Transmitter Address (TA) — the device that transmitted this frame, (3) Destination Address (DA) — the ultimate destination, (4) Source Address (SA) — the original sender. In a typical AP-to-client frame, DA=client MAC, SA=remote host MAC, BSSID=AP MAC. This is more complex than Ethernet's simple src/dst pair.
Section 1

802.11 WLAN Overview

IEEE 802.11 is the Layer 2 protocol for wireless LANs. Unlike Ethernet (cable), 802.11 uses radio frequencies and requires explicit medium access control (CSMA/CA — collision avoidance, not the detection used in Ethernet).

802.11 adds a management layer not present in Ethernet for wireless-specific functions: discovery, authentication, association. The 802.11 frame has more fields than Ethernet: Frame Control (type/subtype, flags), Duration/ID (medium reservation), four address fields, Sequence Control, QoS Control (optional), HT Control (optional), Frame Body, FCS.

Monitor Mode is required for full WLAN visibility Monitor Mode is required to see all 802.11 management and control frames. Without monitor mode, you only see data frames addressed to your MAC. To see Beacons, Probes, Auth, and Association frames, you must use monitor mode.
🧠
MENTAL NOTE
802.11 = Layer 2 wireless. Three frame types: Management (connection control), Control (medium access), Data (user data). 4 address fields (vs 2 Ethernet). Monitor Mode = see ALL frames. Managed Mode = only frames to your MAC. Filter: wlan. CSMA/CA = collision avoidance (different from Ethernet CSMA/CD).

💭 THINK FIRST · 802.11 FRAME TYPES

802.11 management frames are the control plane of wireless networking — they handle everything before a client can send data. Knowing which management frame subtypes correspond to which connection stages lets you trace exactly where a wireless connection fails.

  • An attacker is sending deauthentication frames to disconnect users. Why is this possible with standard WPA2, and what standard was designed to fix it?
  • What does a Beacon frame contain and why does it need to be sent every 100ms?
Q1 — DEAUTH ATTACKS AND 802.11W
In WPA2 without 802.11w (Management Frame Protection), management frames including Deauthentication are NOT authenticated or encrypted. Any device can forge a Deauth frame with a spoofed AP source MAC, and clients will disconnect immediately when they receive it — because clients have no way to verify the frame came from the real AP. 802.11w (Management Frame Protection, also called Protected Management Frames or PMF) encrypts certain management frames including Deauth and Disassociation. WPA3 mandates 802.11w.
Q2 — BEACON FRAME CONTENTS AND INTERVAL
Beacon frames contain: SSID (network name), BSSID (AP MAC), supported data rates, beacon interval, DTIM interval, capabilities (WPA/WPA2/WPA3 info, 802.11n/ac/ax support), timestamp for synchronization. Beacons are sent every 100ms (102.4ms precisely) because: clients in power-save mode wake up at beacon intervals to receive buffered frames; this interval also lets clients discover APs through passive scanning without actively probing. Without regular beacons, clients couldn't discover or maintain awareness of available APs.
Section 2

802.11 Frame Types

Three 802.11 frame type categories:

(1) Management frames — control the wireless relationship. Key subtypes: Beacon (AP announces itself, 100ms interval), Probe Request (client scans for APs), Probe Response (AP replies), Authentication Request/Response (pre-association auth), Association Request/Response (client joins), Reassociation (roaming to new AP), Disassociation, Deauthentication.

(2) Control frames — assist frame delivery. Key subtypes: RTS/CTS (hidden node collision avoidance), ACK (Layer 2 ack for every unicast frame), Block ACK, PS-Poll (power save).

(3) Data frames — carry user payload.

Deauth frames are forgeable without 802.11w Deauthentication frames are NOT protected in WPA2 without 802.11w. Forged Deauth frames from an attacker will disconnect any client. This is how Wi-Fi deauth attacks work. WPA3 mandates Management Frame Protection (802.11w) to prevent this.
🧠
MENTAL NOTE
Management: Beacon (AP heartbeat 100ms), Probe (scan), Auth, Assoc, Deauth (forgeable in WPA2). Control: RTS/CTS, ACK (every unicast). Data: user payload. Deauth forgeable without 802.11w. Filter beacons: wlan.fc.type_subtype==0x08. Filter deauth: wlan.fc.type_subtype==0x0c.

💭 THINK FIRST · WLAN ASSOCIATION PROCESS

The WLAN association process has more steps than wired Ethernet — it includes authentication and key derivation that are invisible at the application layer but visible in a wireless capture. The 4-Way Handshake (four EAPOL frames) is the step where session encryption keys are generated, and capturing it is the prerequisite for decrypting WPA2 traffic.

  • You capture a WLAN session and see four EAPOL frames after the Association Response. What are these frames and why do they matter for traffic decryption?
  • If you have the Wi-Fi password (PSK) and captured the 4-Way Handshake, what Wireshark feature lets you decrypt the WPA2 traffic?
Q1 — THE 4-WAY HANDSHAKE (EAPOL FRAMES)
The four EAPOL frames are the WPA2 4-Way Handshake — the protocol that derives the session encryption keys (PTK and GTK) from the PSK or EAP credentials. Message 1 (AP→Client): AP sends ANonce (a random number). Message 2 (Client→AP): Client sends SNonce + MIC, proving it knows the PSK. Message 3 (AP→Client): AP sends GTK (group key) + MIC. Message 4 (Client→AP): Client confirms. The derived PTK is the unique per-session encryption key for that client's unicast traffic. Without capturing the 4-Way Handshake, you cannot decrypt WPA2 traffic even if you know the PSK.
Q2 — DECRYPTING WPA2 TRAFFIC IN WIRESHARK
Preferences → Protocols → IEEE 802.11 → Decryption Keys → Add key with type "wpa-pwd" and value "password:SSID" (e.g., "mysecret:MyWifi"). Wireshark will use the captured handshake plus the PSK to derive the PTK and decrypt the traffic in the capture.
Section 3

WLAN Association Process

WLAN connection process:

  1. Passive scanning — client listens for Beacon frames.
  2. Active scanning — client sends Probe Request broadcast; APs respond with Probe Response.
  3. Open System Authentication — client sends Authentication Request; AP sends Authentication Response (formality in WPA2, no real security check here).
  4. Association — client sends Association Request with capabilities and RSN Information Element (WPA2 parameters); AP sends Association Response with AID.
  5. 4-Way Handshake (EAPOL frames) — derives PTK (per-client unicast key) and GTK (group/broadcast key) from PSK + random nonces.
  6. Client can now send encrypted data frames.
Capturing the 4-Way Handshake Capture the 4-Way Handshake by starting Wireshark in monitor mode BEFORE the client associates. If you miss the handshake, you can force a new one by sending a deauth frame or disconnecting/reconnecting the client. With the PSK + handshake, Wireshark decrypts the traffic: Preferences → Protocols → IEEE 802.11 → Decryption Keys.
🧠
MENTAL NOTE
WLAN steps: Passive scan (Beacons) → Active scan (Probe Req/Resp) → Auth (open, formality) → Association (Assoc Req/Resp) → 4-Way Handshake (4 EAPOL frames = key derivation) → Data. PTK = per-client unicast key. GTK = group/broadcast key. Must capture handshake to decrypt. Add WPA key: Prefs → IEEE 802.11 → Decryption Keys.

💭 THINK FIRST · WLAN ANALYSIS AND TROUBLESHOOTING

WLAN problems show up as recognizable patterns in the management frames. Repeated Deauths mean disconnection events (attack or AP bug). High retry rates indicate weak signal or interference. Roaming loops mean coverage gaps. Each problem has a specific filter and specific frames to examine.

  • You see a high rate of type_subtype 0x0c (Deauthentication) frames from the same source MAC targeting multiple different clients. What might this indicate?
  • Wireless → WLAN Statistics shows a client with 40% retry rate. What does this indicate?
Q1 — DEAUTH STORM SIGNATURE
This is the classic signature of a wireless deauthentication attack. One source MAC sending Deauth frames to many clients in rapid succession indicates an attacker using a deauth tool to disconnect all clients from the network. Check whether the source MAC matches a known AP's BSSID — if not, it's a forged Deauth attack. If it matches an AP BSSID, it could be a buggy or misbehaving AP.
Q2 — HIGH RETRY RATE
A 40% retry rate means 40% of transmitted frames required retransmission at the Layer 2 level — the receiver didn't acknowledge them within the retry window. Causes: weak signal (client too far from AP), interference (competing 2.4GHz devices, neighboring APs on overlapping channels), or multipath propagation issues. At 40%, the effective throughput is severely degraded. Resolution: move the client closer to the AP, change to a non-overlapping channel, or add AP coverage.

WLAN Analysis and Troubleshooting

WLAN Statistics: Wireless → WLAN Statistics shows per-AP and per-client breakdown: packet counts, retry rates, signal strength (if in capture). High retry rate = signal or interference problem. Reassociation frequency = roaming behavior. Deauth frequency = instability or attack.

WLAN Statistics — retry rate interpretation Wireless → WLAN Statistics shows per-client retry rates. High retry rates (>10%) indicate interference or weak signal. Check which clients have high rates and correlate with their physical location relative to APs.
