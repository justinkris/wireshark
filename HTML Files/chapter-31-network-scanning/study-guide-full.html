Detect Scanning and Discovery Processes

Identifying ARP sweeps, TCP/UDP/IP port scans, and OS fingerprinting with Wireshark

"Identifying discovery and reconnaissance processes in a timely manner may thwart the eventual attack."
MY QUESTIONS▾
MY NOTES
💭THINK FIRST · DISCOVERY AND RECONNAISSANCE▾

Discovery and reconnaissance processes are used to identify hosts, locate services, and learn operating system versions before launching an attack. ARP scans find local hosts; ICMP pings find routable hosts; TCP/UDP port scans find services.

  • Why would an attacker prefer an ARP scan to discover local hosts rather than an ICMP ping sweep?
  • What is the limitation of ARP scanning that makes it insufficient for large-network reconnaissance?
Q1: Firewalls often block ICMP pings, but ARP responses cannot be disabled — that would break TCP/IP communications. ARP scans can therefore discover local devices that are completely hidden from ICMP-based sweeps.
Q2: ARP is a non-routable protocol — ARP packets cannot cross a router or any layer 3 device. ARP scanning can only discover devices on the same local network segment as the scanner. For routable network reconnaissance, ICMP or TCP/UDP scans are required.

The Purpose of Discovery and Reconnaissance

Just as a criminal may investigate the workings of a bank before robbing it, malicious programs and processes may investigate open ports and working hosts before attempting an exploit. Identifying these discovery and reconnaissance processes in a timely manner may thwart the eventual attack. Nmap is one of the most popular tools used to discover network devices and services.

Detect ARP Scans (aka ARP Sweeps)

ARP scans are used to find local hosts only because ARP packets are not routable — ARP packets do not have an IP header. The Nmap parameter to run an ARP scan is -PR (referred to as an ARP ping), but this parameter is rarely used since Nmap automatically uses ARP scan whenever it can (e.g. when the target is on the same Ethernet segment as the source). Common ARP scan processes send ARP requests to the broadcast MAC address (0xff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff).

The advantage of running an ARP scan is that you can discover local devices that may be hidden from other discovery methods by a firewall. You cannot disable ARP responses — that would "break" the TCP/IP communications system. ARP scans will not cross a router. If you detect an ARP scan taking place, the source and targets will be on the same network you are capturing traffic on.

Detect ICMP Ping Sweeps

There are three possible variations of ping sweeps: ICMP-based (Type 8 Echo Request / Type 0 Echo Reply), TCP ping scans (destination port 7, Echo port), and UDP ping scans (destination port 7, Echo port). The standard ICMP-based ping sweep worked great for many years until firewalls were configured to block these types of ICMP packets.

ICMP-based ping sweeps are easy to detect with a simple filter for icmp.type==8 || icmp.type==0. The Nmap syntax for an ICMP-based ping sweep is -sP.

💭THINK FIRST · TCP PORT SCANS▾

TCP scans use SYN packets to discover open ports. Half-open (stealth) scans send SYN but reset before completing the handshake — the target sees no completed connection. Full connect scans complete the handshake.

  • Why is a TCP half-open scan called a "stealth scan" — what makes it stealthier than a full connect scan?
  • What does a RST response versus no response versus ICMP Type 3 response mean when scanning a TCP port?
Q1: In a half-open scan, the scanner sends SYN but resets (RST) upon receipt of a SYN/ACK instead of completing the handshake. The target sees an incomplete connection that is never fully established — it will not appear in the list of open connections on the target, making the scan harder to detect via connection state monitoring.
Q2: RST = port is closed. No response = port may be open or filtered (cannot distinguish intentional filtering from packet loss). ICMP Type 3 with Code 1, 2, 3, 9, 10 or 13 = port is firewalled and explicitly blocked.

Detect Various Types of TCP Port Scans

Port scans are used to discover targets and/or services offered on a target. The majority of popular services run over TCP. Common target TCP ports include: FTP (21), SSH (22), Telnet (23), SMTP (25), HTTP (80), POP (110), NTP (123), Endpoint Mapper (135), NetBIOS (139), HTTPS (443), SMB/DS (445), MSSQL (1433), VNC (5900), HTTP Alternate (8080).

TCP Half-Open Scan (Stealth Scan)

Nmap uses a TCP half-open (a.k.a. stealth scan) by default. It does not finish up the three-way handshake to make a complete connection if a port is open. Upon receipt of the SYN/ACK from the target, the host running Nmap will generate a TCP Reset to terminate the connection attempt. Since the scanner does not complete the three-way handshake, a target can look at their list of open connections and the scanning host will not show up — hence the name "stealth scan." The half-open TCP scan is desired for stealthiness and resource preservation on the target.

A TCP Reset response indicates that the target port is closed. If no response is received, we cannot assume the port is open or closed. An ICMP Destination Unreachable (Type 3) response with a Code 1, 2, 3, 9, 10 or 13 indicates that the port is probably firewalled.

Figure 359. TCP scans in close proximity are easy to identify — look for high numbers of RSTs or SYN/ACKs without data transfer.

TCP Full Connect Scan

TCP full connect scans complete the three-way handshake after receiving a SYN/ACK packet from an open port. Just as with TCP half-open scans, these can be difficult to detect with Wireshark unless the scans are in close proximity. An unusually high number of RSTs or a high number of SYN/ACKs without data transfer are strong indications that a TCP scan is underway.

💭THINK FIRST · NULL, XMAS, ACK, UDP SCANS▾

Null scans (no TCP flags), Xmas scans (URG+FIN+PUSH), ACK scans (firewall probing), and UDP scans each have distinct signatures. The response behavior differs between open, closed, and firewalled ports.

  • For a Null scan or Xmas scan, what does "no response" mean — and how is this different from the same "no response" in a SYN scan?
  • Why would an attacker run an ACK scan rather than a SYN scan to probe for open ports?
Q1: In Null and Xmas scans: no response = port is open OR filtered (cannot distinguish). TCP RST = port is closed. ICMP Type 3 codes 1/2/3/9/10/13 = firewalled. In a SYN scan: no response also means open|filtered but the SYN scan can get a SYN/ACK as confirmation of open — Null/Xmas scans cannot get this positive confirmation from open ports.
Q2: ACK scans are used to check firewall rules, not to find open ports. An ACK scan sends a TCP ACK with no preceding handshake. A TCP RST response means the port is unfiltered (the firewall allowed the packet through). An ICMP Type 3 response or no response means the port is likely filtered. The ACK scan reveals the firewall ruleset.

Other Scan Types and ICMP Scans

Null Scans

Null scans use an unusual TCP packet format — none of the TCP flags are set. No response to a null scan indicates that the port is either open or filtered. A TCP Reset response indicates the port is closed. An ICMP Destination Unreachable (Type 3) response with a Code 1, 2, 3, 9, 10 or 13 indicates that the port is probably firewalled. To detect null scans, consider creating a coloring rule or display filter for tcp.flags==0x000.

📷Figure 361 — open trace file to view
Figure 361. Null scans do not have any TCP flags set — filter: tcp.flags==0x000.

Xmas Scans

Xmas scans have the URG, FIN and PUSH flags set. Response interpretation is identical to Null scans. The Nmap syntax for an Xmas scan is -sX. To detect Xmas scans, create a coloring rule or display filter: tcp.flags==0x029.

📷Figure 362 — open trace file to view
Figure 362. Xmas scans have the Urgent, Push and Finish flags set — filter: tcp.flags==0x029.

ACK Scans

ACK scans are typically used to check firewall rules to see if ports are explicitly blocked. ACK scans are not used to identify open ports unless the window scan technique (-sW) is used as well. An ACK scan sends a TCP packet with only the ACK flag bit set to 1 — there is no TCP handshake preceding the ACK scan. A TCP RST response indicates the port is unfiltered. An ICMP Destination Unreachable response (Type 3) or no response indicates the port is likely filtered. The Nmap syntax for an ACK scan is -sA.

📷Figure 363 — open trace file to view
Figure 363. ACK scans are used to check firewall rules and identify blocked ports.

UDP Port Scans

Although the majority of popular services run over TCP, certain very interesting services run over UDP: DNS (53), SNMP (161/162), DHCP (67/68), SIP (5060), Microsoft Endpoint Mapper (135), NetBIOS Name Service (137/139). An ICMP Destination Unreachable/Port Unreachable response indicates that the service is not available on the target. No response indicates that the service might be available or might just be filtered. The Nmap syntax for a UDP scan is -sU.

📷Figure 364 — open trace file to view
Figure 364. UDP scans to closed ports trigger ICMP Destination Unreachable/Port Unreachable responses.

IP Protocol Scans

IP protocol scans are designed to locate services running directly over IP. For example, an IP scan can locate a device that supports EIGRP. When a protocol is not supported on a target, the target may respond with an ICMP Destination Unreachable, Protocol Unreachable response (Type 3/Code 2). The Nmap syntax for an IP protocol scan is -sO. To detect IP protocol scans: icmp.type==3 && icmp.code==2.

📷Figure 365 — open trace file to view
Figure 365. IP scans are used to locate services that run directly over IP — look for ICMP Protocol Unreachable responses.

Idle Scans

Idle scans are used when a scanner is prohibited from talking directly to a target. The scanner uses a "zombie" host as a proxy. The scanner probes the zombie IP ID field to determine if the target port responded to the zombie. If the zombie IP ID increments by 1, the target port is closed. If it increments by 2, the target port is open (the zombie received a SYN/ACK and sent a RST, incrementing the IP ID). The Nmap syntax for an idle scan is -sI zombie_host.

Analyze Traceroute Path Discovery

A common use of ICMP is as a path discovery mechanism using ICMP Echo Request (Type 8) packets with incrementing TTL values. Each router along the path decrements the TTL by 1. When the packet arrives at a router with TTL=1, the router discards the packet and sends an ICMP Time Exceeded in Transit (Type 11) packet to the originator. Packets with a TTL value lower than 5 are considered suspicious. Wireshark includes a default coloring rule for packets with low TTL values.

Figure 368. An ICMP-based traceroute depends on ICMP Time Exceeded in Transit responses to discover routers.

To detect ICMP-based, UDP-based or TCP-based traceroute, consider creating a coloring rule or display filter for (icmp.type==11) && (icmp.code==0). This coloring rule must be placed above the default ICMP Errors coloring rule. For ICMP Router Solicitation/Advertisement detection: icmp.type==9 || icmp.type==10.

💭THINK FIRST · OS FINGERPRINTING▾

OS fingerprinting identifies the operating system of a target host. Passive fingerprinting uses existing traffic (HTTP UserAgent, TCP options). Active fingerprinting sends crafted packets to elicit OS-specific responses.

  • What makes HTTP UserAgent-based OS fingerprinting unreliable for definitive OS identification?
  • How does Nmap active OS fingerprinting detect operating systems through multiple crafted probe packets?
Q1: The UserAgent string can be easily spoofed by configuring the browser or using a proxy. Additional OS fingerprinting techniques should always be used in conjunction with UserAgent-based passive fingerprinting for confirmation.
Q2: Nmap sends a series of crafted packets (TCP SYN with unusual options, ICMP Echo with specific payloads, TCP flags in unusual combinations) and compares the responses to the nmap-os-db file which contains known response signatures for thousands of OS versions. The unique combination of TCP window size, TTL, MSS, and option handling identifies the OS.

Use Wireshark for Passive OS Fingerprinting

OS fingerprinting is the process of determining the operating system of a target through either active scanning or passive listening. Wireshark can be used as a passive listening device. Trace files taken by Wireshark can be used to make some assumptions regarding the operating system running on hosts. For example, if traffic travels to and from ports 135, 137, 139 and 445 on a host, you can make some basic assumptions that the host is a Windows host.

Passive OS Fingerprinting via HTTP UserAgent

HTTP GET requests contain a UserAgent definition. For example, to detect HTTP communications from a host running Firefox, apply the display filter http contains "Firefox". Note that this string filter is case sensitive. The UserAgent information includes the browser application name and version number, the operating system information, and additional capabilities. To view the UserAgent string generated by your browser, type javascript:alert(navigator.userAgent) in the browser address bar. This line can be spoofed, so additional OS fingerprinting techniques should be used in conjunction.

Detect Active OS Fingerprinting

Active OS fingerprinting can be much more efficient than passive OS fingerprinting, but it can also be detected by listening applications such as Wireshark. Nmap is an excellent example of an OS fingerprinting tool. The Nmap parameter to run OS fingerprinting with verbosity and version detection is -sV -O -v. Examining Nmap OS fingerprinting provides numerous signatures of its traffic:

  • ICMP Echo Request (Type 8) with no payload
  • ICMP Echo Request (Type 8) with 120 or 150 byte payload of 0x00s
  • ICMP Timestamp Request with Originate Timestamp value set to 0
  • TCP SYN with 40 byte options area
  • TCP SYN with Window Scale Shift Count set to 10
  • TCP SYN with Maximum Segment Size set to 256
  • TCP packet with options and SYN, FIN, PSH and URG bits set
  • TCP packet with options and no flags set

Another popular OS fingerprinting technique uses a series of ICMP packets in close proximity: Type 13 ICMP Timestamp Requests, Type 15 ICMP Information Requests, and Type 17 ICMP Address Mask Requests. To detect these: icmp.type==13 || icmp.type==15 || icmp.type==17.

Identify Spoofed Addresses in Scans

Attackers and scanners may use MAC or IP address spoofing to hide their actual hardware or network addresses. In a Denial of Service flood style attack, where the attacker is not relying on two-way communications, they may spoof their MAC or IP address. Nmap supports MAC address spoofing with the --spoof-mac option and IP address spoofing with the -S parameter.

Case Study

Case Study 1
Learning the Conficker Lesson
Submitted by: Bill Bach, Goldstar Software

Bill Bach (Goldstar Software) was called when a school lost Internet access after a library IT department pulled the plug on the shared network, citing "virus-like" activity. Despite scanning all school PCs and finding them clean, the library IT staff refused to reconnect until the two machines they blamed (both of which had been offline for months) were cleaned.

Once the school was reconnected with Wireshark monitoring the link, the team immediately saw a machine at the library trying to connect to newly-booted lab computers — and then scanning sequential IP addresses every quarter second.

They immediately cut the bridge link on the school side and asked the library to remove their infected machine. Before reconnecting, they set up a NAT router to create a completely separate subnet for the school, protecting school computers from library network devices. The lesson: always segregate your network from your network partners, even with a simple NAT device, and monitor your network boundary periodically with Wireshark.

TL;DR — Chapter 31 Summary

Discovery and reconnaissance processes are used to locate hosts and services on a network. The ultimate purpose may be to map out network devices or locate vulnerable systems for an impending attack. Recognizing these processes can help prevent network breaches.

Various scan techniques can be used to discover network devices, such as ARP scans (local only), ping sweeps, UDP scans, and TCP scans. Each of these scans generates recognizable traffic patterns on the network. TCP half-open scans (Nmap default) use SYN/RST without completing the handshake. TCP full connect scans complete the handshake. Null scans (tcp.flags==0x000) and Xmas scans (tcp.flags==0x029) use unusual TCP flag combinations. ACK scans probe firewall rules. UDP scans trigger ICMP Port Unreachable on closed ports.

A thorough understanding of how each of these protocols works for normal network communications will help you identify when these protocols may be used for malicious purposes. Creating coloring rules for unusual TCP flag combinations, unusual ICMP payloads, and suspicious packet sizes makes scanning traffic immediately visible.

Review Questions

Q31.1. What is the purpose of discovery and reconnaissance processes?
A: Discovery and reconnaissance processes are used to identify hosts on the network, locate network services, learn operating system versions running on hosts and any other information on network devices.
Q31.2. What is the limitation of ARP scanning?
A: Because ARP is a non-routable protocol, ARP scanning can only find local devices. The advantage of ARP scanning is that the process can locate devices that block ICMP pings through firewall use.
Q31.3. What are the two reasons someone may run a TCP port scan?
A: TCP port scans can be used to discover targets (is this host online?) and to discover services offered on a target (what ports are open?).
Q31.4. How can you differentiate between a TCP full connect scan and a TCP half-open scan?
A: A TCP full connect scan completes the three-way handshake (SYN, SYN/ACK, ACK). A TCP half-open scan sends a RST upon receipt of the SYN/ACK, preventing the handshake from completing.
Q31.5. What type of device may send an ICMP Destination Unreachable response to a TCP connection attempt?
A: A firewall may send an ICMP Destination Unreachable (Type 3) with codes 1, 2, 3, 9, 10 or 13 to indicate that the port is blocked or administratively prohibited.
Q31.6. What process can be detected by an unusual number of IP packets that contain a low TTL value?
A: A traceroute operation can be detected by an unusual number of IP packets that contain a low TTL value — each probe packet has a progressively incremented TTL starting from 1.
Q31.7. What are the two distinct functions of application mapping?
A: Application mapping relies on two distinct functions: probing (proactively sending packets to a target to generate responses) and matching (comparing responses to predefined response patterns to identify the service discovered).
Q31.8. What is the advantage of performing passive OS fingerprinting with Wireshark?
A: Passive OS fingerprinting with Wireshark does not generate any traffic — it is completely undetectable by the target because you are only observing existing traffic on the network.
