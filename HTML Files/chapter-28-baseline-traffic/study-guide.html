💭 THINK FIRST · WHY BASELINING MATTERS

A baseline converts subjective "something seems wrong" into objective "this metric is 5x above its documented normal value." Without a baseline, you're making qualitative judgments with no reference — you can't definitively say whether 15% ARP traffic is a problem without knowing that normal for your network is 0.3%.

  • A network engineer says "we don't baseline — we only capture when something breaks." What is wrong with this approach?
  • You baseline your network on Tuesday morning. On Thursday the traffic patterns look different. Before concluding the network changed, what should you do?
Q1 — PROBLEMS WITH REACTIVE-ONLY CAPTURE
Three fundamental problems: (1) Without a baseline, you have no reference for what "normal" looks like — you can't tell whether observed traffic is anomalous or expected. (2) Security anomalies (malware beaconing, data exfiltration) may be present but indistinguishable from normal traffic because you've never documented what normal is. (3) When a problem occurs, you have no before/after comparison — you can describe the current state but can't prove what changed. Baselines enable three critical capabilities: troubleshooting (before/after comparison), security (anomaly detection), and capacity planning (trend analysis).
Q2 — COMPARING ACROSS DIFFERENT TIME PERIODS
Before concluding the network changed, capture at the same time period (Tuesday morning) to see if the pattern repeats. Traffic varies by time of day, day of week, and business cycle. A Tuesday morning baseline compared to Thursday afternoon will show differences due to time-of-day variation, not necessarily network changes. A proper baseline includes captures at multiple times: peak hours, off-peak, and overnight. Compare like-with-like (same time period, same capture location).
Section 1

Why Baselining Matters

A baseline is a documented record of normal network behavior. It provides the reference point that makes every subsequent analysis meaningful. Without it, analysis is subjective; with it, analysis is objective and defensible.

A baseline serves three distinct purposes:

  1. Troubleshooting — compare a problem capture against the baseline to identify what changed. "Is 15% retransmissions a problem?" becomes "Yes, the baseline shows 0.2%."
  2. Security — detect anomalous traffic by comparing observed behavior against documented expected behavior. Malware beaconing, DNS tunneling, and data exfiltration are most visible as deviations from baseline.
  3. Capacity planning — track traffic growth trends over time. Monthly baselines reveal whether a link is approaching saturation.

A baseline is not a one-time snapshot — it must be captured at multiple times (business peak, off-peak, overnight) and periodically repeated (monthly or quarterly). Without a baseline, you cannot reliably identify what is abnormal.

Capture at multiple times Capture baselines at multiple times of day and multiple days of week. A single morning capture misses afternoon peak traffic, overnight backups, and batch jobs. Security incidents often happen at off-hours when monitoring is minimal.

💭 THINK FIRST · WHAT TO INCLUDE IN A BASELINE

A useful baseline captures enough metrics to answer "is this normal?" for any common analysis question. Missing key metrics — like the TCP retransmission rate — means you can't determine whether a current problem represents a change or was always present.

  • Your baseline shows Host A generating 40% of network traffic. A new capture shows Host A at 60%. Is this necessarily a problem?
  • Why is the TCP retransmission rate an important baseline metric even when nothing appears to be wrong?
Q1 — HOST A TRAFFIC SHARE INCREASE
Not necessarily — context determines whether this is a problem. If Host A is a high-traffic server (file server, database, video streaming server), 40% might be its normal share of traffic, and the increase to 60% might simply reflect increased legitimate usage. To determine if it's a problem: check whether the increase is sustained or a peak, what the nature of the traffic is (normal application traffic vs. unusual protocols), and whether other hosts have correspondingly less traffic (suggesting bandwidth was reallocated) or not (suggesting new traffic was added). The baseline gives you the number; you still need to understand the meaning.
Q2 — WHY BASELINE TCP RETRANSMISSIONS
Every network has some baseline level of TCP retransmissions even when everything is working normally. If your baseline shows 0.3% retransmissions and a problem capture shows 8%, that 25x increase is meaningful evidence. But if you never measured the baseline, you might see 8% and wonder if that's always been the case. The baseline retransmission rate also helps calibrate alert thresholds — a network with inherently noisy links might have 2% as its normal rate, requiring higher thresholds than a clean LAN.

💭 THINK FIRST · NORMAL TRAFFIC METRICS

General-purpose baseline reference ranges exist for common protocol percentages. Knowing these typical values helps you recognize when a new capture shows something worth investigating — even before you have your own network-specific baseline.

  • Your baseline shows DNS at 2% of traffic. A new capture shows DNS at 22%. What are two possible causes — one benign and one malicious?
  • Normal ARP in your baseline is 0.3% of traffic. A new capture shows ARP at 15%. What are the two most common causes?
Q1 — DNS SPIKE FROM 2% TO 22%
Benign cause: a new application was deployed that performs frequent DNS lookups (e.g., a monitoring tool checking many endpoints), a DNS server failure causing clients to retry repeatedly, or a large number of new clients performing initial DNS lookups simultaneously. Malicious cause: DNS tunneling malware or a C2 beacon using DNS queries to communicate with a remote server. DGA-based malware generates hundreds of DNS queries for randomly-generated domain names (most returning NXDOMAIN). Investigate by filtering DNS and checking: which host is generating the queries, what domains are being queried, and what percentage are returning NXDOMAIN.
Q2 — ARP SPIKE FROM 0.3% TO 15%
Cause 1: ARP broadcast storm — a misconfigured device or network loop is generating excessive ARP traffic. Check if the ARP requests are from many sources (broadcast storm) or one source (scanner or misconfigured device). Cause 2: Network scanning — a scanner (nmap, automated vulnerability scanner) is performing an ARP sweep to discover live hosts on the subnet. Filter arp.opcode == 1 (who-has requests) and check if one MAC is sending ARP to all IPs in rapid sequence.

💭 THINK FIRST · CAPTURING BASELINES

A baseline is only useful if it's captured correctly and documented thoroughly. Missing metadata (when captured, where, which interface, any unusual events) makes the baseline uninterpretable when pulled out months later during an investigation.

  • What information should you record alongside the .pcapng file when creating a baseline?
  • You want to compare this week's capture against last month's baseline. Which Wireshark statistics view gives you the easiest comparison of protocol distribution?
Q1 — METADATA TO RECORD WITH A BASELINE
Essential metadata: (1) Date and time of capture start and end. (2) Day of week and whether this was a typical business day or unusual period. (3) Capture location: which network segment, which switch/router, which VLAN, physical location. (4) Capture interface description and any specific NIC/driver details. (5) Capture filter applied (if any — if filtered, the baseline is partial). (6) Any unusual events during the capture period (maintenance window, peak sale period, known incident). (7) Wireshark version used. (8) Name of the analyst who captured it. Without this metadata, a baseline file from 6 months ago is essentially uninterpretable.
Q2 — COMPARING PROTOCOL DISTRIBUTION
Statistics → Protocol Hierarchy. Run it on both the baseline capture and the new capture (using separate Wireshark windows), then compare the percentage columns side by side. The hierarchy shows the complete protocol distribution as percentages, making it easy to spot which protocols have grown or shrunk. For a more automated comparison, you can export both as CSV and compare in a spreadsheet.
Section 4

Capturing and Documenting Baselines

The value of a baseline depends entirely on how it's collected and documented. A file with no context is nearly useless six months later.

Baseline capture procedure:

  1. Choose the right location — key network segments (core, distribution, internet edge, critical servers). Use a TAP for accuracy; avoid SPAN port packet drops.
  2. Capture at representative times — business peak (9–11am), midday, end of day (4–6pm), overnight (2–4am). Capture on multiple days including weekends if relevant.
  3. Sufficient duration — minimum 1 hour at peak, 30 minutes off-peak. Longer is better for establishing statistical significance.
  4. Record metadata — date/time, location, interface, VLAN, any unusual events during the capture window.
  5. Run statistics — Protocol Hierarchy, top 10 Conversations by bytes, IO Graph min/max/avg, TCP error counts via tcp.analysis.flags.
  6. Save as .pcapng with a descriptive filename (e.g., core-sw-morning-peak-2024-10-15.pcapng).
  7. Document in a baseline record — a simple text or spreadsheet file alongside the .pcapng capturing the key statistics and context.
Document results, not just the capture file Document baseline results in a simple table — protocol percentages, top 5 talker IPs, peak bandwidth, average response times. Store it with the .pcapng file. This document is what you pull out when investigating an incident — you don't want to re-open and re-analyze the .pcapng under pressure.
🧠
MENTAL NOTE
Baseline procedure: TAP at key segments → multiple time periods (peak+off-peak+overnight) → 1 hour minimum → record all metadata → save .pcapng with descriptive filename + accompanying text document. Run: Protocol Hierarchy + Conversations + IO Graph stats. Compare future captures with Statistics → Protocol Hierarchy (side-by-side Wireshark windows).
